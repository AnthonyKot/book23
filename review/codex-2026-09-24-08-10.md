# Book 23 review — Chapters 08–10 — 2026-09-24

Scope: primary-source, prose, brief and existing-code review. Findings for each chapter are

## Chapter 08 — Left On

Mechanism: UpGuard found that Power Apps portal lists with OData enabled could return rows to
anonymous callers when list-level “Enable Table Permissions” remained at its then-unsafe
default, even if the underlying table had permissions. That is a defensible API8 configuration
failure: a switch controlled whether an existing permission check applied.

Findings before edits, ranked:

- **BLOCK:** The Chapter 8 brief calls the settings field `AllowedOrigins`, while the fixed
  CONTEXT value is `CORSOrigins`. Lane A needs one field name. Its debug-error example also
  needs an internal reason source: Chapter 1's `LoadInvoiceFor(user,id)` returns only an invoice
  and a Boolean, so the named tenant cannot appear in an error body without another specified
  store operation.
- **BLOCK:** Exercise C's fixed column says the build fails, not what the same request returns
  in fixed mode. The chapter's own worked answer says fixed production returns an opaque 404.
  Separate the expected rejected-settings validation from the both-mode HTTP outcome; an
  intentionally failed assertion must not be counted as a passing security test.
- **FIX:** UpGuard's “Microsoft Response” section says Portal Checker had been released and
  table-permission defaults for new portals were planned; its conclusion speaks of the default
  as changed. The chapter's precise “had changed ... by 23 August” chronology is stronger than
  that mixed record supports. Say Microsoft announced the default change, without a rollout date.
- **FIX:** The source says list-level table permissions and custom table permissions were
  ignored until the list switch was enabled; “every other permission” is broader than it shows.
- **NOTE:** The Chapter 5 prefix guard is absent in both modes; the copied `Public` declaration
  stands alone and is genuinely a wrong value, while the debug error body reopens Chapter 1's
  opaque 404. The hinge is “The check was there, wired correctly, and switched off.” A is the
  plausible decoy; A/B differ by body under the same `Public` level, and C/C' differ by one
  Boolean with opposite vulnerable response bodies. The cold-read risk is the mixed “build
  fails” versus “request returns 404” language in C.

Design answers before edits: **visible pair — yes** for A/B and C/C' in the vulnerable build,
but C's fixed result is not a served response; **Lane A ready — no** because the settings field,
denial-reason source and config-validation/HTTP-test split are unspecified; **State of Ledger —
yes on access order and status**, with `PublicRoutes` correctly layering on Chapter 5's declared
levels and same-tenant role denials staying 403.

Changed: `chapters/08-left-on.md` narrows the table-permission and Microsoft-default claims,
states where the debug-only denial reason comes from, and gives C an actual fixed-mode opaque
404 while keeping a separate configuration assertion. `briefs/08.md` now uses CONTEXT's
`CORSOrigins`, defines `RouteKey`, names an internal store reason helper without bypassing
Chapter 1 in handlers, and gives the expected validation result rather than an intentionally
failing Go test. It removes the stale Chapter 5 prefix-guard assumption. `CONTEXT.md` registers
the web-app CORS origin and the two health-route behaviors. The original UpGuard disclosure is
archived with ten located claims in `checks/claims/08.tsv`.

Design answers after edits: **visible pair — yes**, A/B differ by response detail under the
same `Public` declaration and C/C' differ by the `Debug` value while the fixed request remains
opaque. **Lane A ready — yes as a specification**, with the debug-only reason helper and the
settings validator's expected errors separated from served requests. **State of Ledger — yes**:
no prefix guard is assumed; Chapter 5's access declaration and Chapter 1's loader still run.

Validation: the one source hash matches the manifest and ten seven-column claims point to that
archive; `go test ./...`, `./verify.sh`, and `git diff --check` passed. The verifier still executes
only the Chapter 1/9 Go cases and reports 31 pending code blocks, so Chapter 8's proposed
settings assertions are not yet executable.

Left for the author and Lane A: implement the health routes, debug error writer and production
settings validator, then assert each exercise row in both modes. The UpGuard article does not
date the new-default rollout precisely, so the book leaves that date unstated.

Verdict: **ready for the author's prose read, not yet executable**. The decisive remaining gap
is the absent Chapter 8 service and both-mode tests.

## Chapter 09 — Deprecated Is a Label

Mechanism: ACMA's filed, redacted concise statement alleges that an internet-facing Optus
Target Domain stayed dormant but undecommissioned, missed the Main Domain's 2021 access-control
repair, and was used in the 2022 attack. The allegation directly supports API9's inventory
management lesson without revealing the redacted system names or an exploit payload.

Findings before edits, ranked:

- **BLOCK:** Chapter 4's completed fix places the 30/min tenant-and-route lookup budget at the
  gateway *and in the shared handler*; its brief applies it to both v1 and v2. Chapter 9 says
  the staging host lets the same lookup “run at full speed” because its gateway rule missed the
  budget. The gateway copy can be bypassed, but the shared handler still limits the request;
  the `/v1/invoices/104` path in Chapter 9 is also not the email lookup Chapter 4 budgeted.
  Narrow this paragraph without reopening Chapter 4 or weakening its fixed behavior.
- **FIX:** The filing says the Target Domain was internet-facing from 20 April 2017, and both
  domains were in the vulnerable internet-facing state by June 2020. “In June 2020 both domains
  became internet-facing” implies the Target first went live then and conflicts with the prior
  sentence. Claim 09-11 needs the same clarification.
- **FIX:** The opening groups residential addresses with fields every customer lost. ACMA's
  ¶27 says all customers had names, email, birth date and phone accessed, while address and
  identity-document access affected subsets. The sentence and 09-14 locator should say so.
- **FIX:** The filing's court order records the concise statement filed on 22 May 2024, but the
  current claims ledger has no row gating “sued in May 2024.” Add one.
- **FIX:** All six exercise cases have two-mode status assertions, and the four expected
  reconciliation findings are tested, but `ch09_test.go` never asserts invoice data in the 200
  responses or its absence in 404 bodies. Add small body assertions to make the Chapter 1 loader
  and retirement behavior visible, as in `ch01_test.go`.
- **NOTE:** All three printed Go blocks match their marked excerpts byte for byte. The example
  reuses `/v1`, `LoadInvoiceFor`, the Chapter 1 route inventory and the Chapter 4 budget; its
  hinge is “A host that is not on your list is a host your fixes do not reach.” A is the plausible
  current-route decoy; D/E are the near-identical staging requests whose vulnerable outcomes
  differ by `/v1/` versus `/v2/`. A cold Go reader can predict the gateway result from its
  `strings.HasPrefix` check, though the rate-limit paragraph currently misstates the cumulative
  state.

Source check: 09-10 through 09-14 were compared with the archived filing's scanned concise
statement (PDF pages 3–6, OCR used for reading). The domain names, 2017/2020 chronology,
trial-and-error wording, response times and information categories are in the cited paragraphs;
the chapter must keep them attributed as allegations.

Design answers before edits: **visible pair — yes**, D returns 200 and E 404 in the vulnerable
build, with both 404 after retirement; **Lane A ready — yes for the implemented pilot**, except
the prose falsely describes the cumulative lookup budget and tests need body assertions;
**State of Ledger — no for that budget paragraph**, while the Chapter 1 loader and Chapter 9
retirement states otherwise agree.

Changed: `chapters/09-deprecated-is-a-label.md` now separates fields accessed for all customers
from address and identity-document subsets, makes June 2020 the point by which both domains
were internet-facing with the error, and says the staging host misses only the gateway copy of
Chapter 4's email-lookup budget, not the shared-handler budget. It distinguishes that email
lookup from this chapter's invoice-by-ID path. `checks/claims/09.tsv` clarifies 09-11 and 09-14
and adds 09-15 for the May 2024 filing. `service/ch09_test.go` now asserts that every 200 exercise
response contains Alice's invoice 104 and that every 404 response excludes invoice data, in
both modes. The three printed Go blocks remained byte-for-byte identical to their excerpts.

Design answers after edits: **visible pair — yes and executed**, D/E have opposite vulnerable
responses and all six cases assert both-mode status and body. **Lane A ready — yes**, with the
Chapter 9 pilot already implemented and tested. **State of Ledger — yes**, Chapter 4's shared
lookup budget and Chapter 1's loader retain their earlier behavior while Chapter 9 retires v1
and the staging host.

Validation: all fifteen seven-column claim rows point to the archived court PDF or ACMA
statement; 09-10 through 09-14 were checked against the filing's scanned pages. The three
printed Go blocks match marked service excerpts byte for byte. `go test ./...`, `./verify.sh`,
and `git diff --check` passed; the verifier exercises all six Chapter 9 cases in both modes and
the four vulnerable/zero fixed inventory findings.

Left for the author: the inventory fixture is intentionally same-process and static; it proves
the comparison logic, not completeness of real gateway configuration, logs, DNS or certificate
records. The chapter already names that limit and the need for outside probing.

Verdict: **ready for the author's read and executable as the Chapter 9 pilot**. The most
important remaining limitation is that its inventory inputs are fixtures rather than an audit
of a deployed gateway.
